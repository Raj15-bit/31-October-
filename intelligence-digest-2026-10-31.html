01

The big picture: the AI bill arrived, and India stood apart

US tech shares fell hard on the cost of AI, oil and inflation stayed high, and Indian shares held up only at the top.

In short
  • The biggest US tech firms showed that AI is now eating their cash, and their shares fell hard.
  • US prices are rising again, so the next US rate move may be up, not down.
  • India beat almost every market in October, but small Indian shares kept falling even on good results.

2 forces met at the end of October. In the US, the firms that drive the AI boom reported results that showed huge spending and thin cash. At the same time US inflation picked up and oil stayed near war highs, so cheap money is not coming back soon.

The AI spending bill

Alphabet, Amazon and Intel lost close to $1 trillion of market value between them in 2 days. The problem was not weak demand. It was that spending on AI chips and data centres now eats more cash than the business brings in.

Reading between the lines, investors are not turning against AI. They are turning against paying for it with borrowed money. That links straight to India: when the biggest US firms borrow to build, US borrowing costs stay high, and that keeps foreign money away from markets like India.

Why good results are not enough

One view is that India is cheap after a long fall and its profits are growing fastest in 3 years, so foreign money must return. The opposite view is that oil near $117 and a US rate rise will keep the rupee weak and money scarce, so even good results get sold. Both sides use the same results season; they disagree on the price of money.

  • Profits — the first 337 companies to report grew adjusted profit by 25.5%, the fastest in 12 quarters.
  • Money — individual investors took out about ₹7,300 crore from shares in October, and 89% of foreign index futures bet on a fall.
  • Price — only 35% of NSE shares trade above their 200-day average (a common test of an uptrend).

02

The price of money: rate rises are back on the table

The US, Europe, Japan and India all lean towards dearer money, while the US bond market quietly gained as shares fell.

In short
  • The US central bank kept rates steady, but markets now expect a rise in December.
  • India's central bank has already raised its rate, and a tax cut for foreign investors is being debated.
  • Money that left tech shares went into US government bonds, which pushed their borrowing cost down.

The Fed waits, but leans towards a rise

The US Federal Reserve, now led by Kevin Warsh, held its rate at 3.75-4.00%. 2 members, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, voted for a rise straight away. The data behind them is firm: the US economy grew fast and prices are rising again.

Bonds were the safe place

When tech shares fell, money moved into US government bonds. The 10-year borrowing cost (the yearly return a lender asks to lend to the US government over that period) fell about 0.35% in October, to near 4.91%.

That was the biggest monthly drop of the year and one of the largest in a decade. A fund holding long US bonds rose almost 5% in October while the main tech fund fell. Reading between the lines, this is fear, not relief: bond prices rose because investors wanted safety, not because inflation is beaten.

Europe and Japan

  • ECB — held at 2.50%; a December rise is largely priced. Christine Lagarde will leave as president on 7 February 2027, about 9 months early.
  • Euro area — grew 0.4% in Q3, twice the forecast, though France shrank. Fitch cut France's outlook to negative.
  • Bank of Japan — held at 1.25% by an 8-1 vote and said the long fight against falling prices is over.
  • Japan's ETF sales — the central bank will sell the share funds it bought, about ¥860 billion a year from January 2027, a pace that would take about 43 years to finish.

India: the rate went up, the rupee stayed weak

The Reserve Bank of India raised the repo rate (the rate at which it lends to banks) to 5.50% at its October meeting and called its new stance "calibrated tightening". Banks passed it on fast; Bank of Baroda repriced its repo-linked loans higher at once.

A Bloomberg column argued that the Fed's pause gives the RBI time to defend the rupee. So India is raising rates to protect its currency, not to cool a hot economy, which is why growth stocks get no help.

The tax that may bring foreign money back

Foreign funds took out about $21 billion from Indian shares in FY26 and $16.5 billion more this year up to 23 October. Jefferies says a cut in long-term capital gains tax (tax on profits from shares held over a year) could bring them back and support the rupee.

  • Revenue at stake — long-term gains tax brought about ₹1.29 lakh crore in FY25; with the securities transaction tax, about 8% of direct taxes.
  • Options — cut the rate for all, cut it only for foreign funds, or tax foreign gains in dollars; an ordinance is possible.
  • Industry — the PHDCCI business body asked for the tax to be scrapped and short-term tax cut to 10%.

One view is that a cut would quickly lift foreign flows, as happened when bond-tax relief brought $9 billion into Indian debt. The opposite view is that tax cuts give a short party, as the 2019 corporate tax cut did, and only growth, value and money supply matter over years. Reading between the lines, the government is unlikely to give up all that revenue, so a cut only for foreign funds is the more likely shape.

03

Oil, ships and war: refiners win, everyone else pays

Tankers are being hit near Hormuz again, fuel margins are at records, and the cost lands in Indian companies' results.

In short
  • More ships were hit near the Strait of Hormuz, so oil prices rose again.
  • Oil refiners made record profits, while airlines, paint makers and drink makers paid more.
  • Gold and silver fell hard even though war risk is high.

Hormuz heats up again

The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow sea lane between Iran and Oman that carries about a fifth of the world's oil. Iran's Revolutionary Guard said "non-compliant" tankers hit mines there, and counts ran from 3 to 8 ships hit in about 48 hours.

  • Ships — a Qatari gas tanker was hit south of Iran's Lavan Island; a burning supertanker reportedly carried over 2 million barrels.
  • Brent — topped $117, and January futures touched $118, a 6-month high.
  • Talks — Pakistan and Qatar are carrying messages between the US and Iran; a report says the US may strike again after the 3 November US elections if talks fail.
  • Cost to the US — the Pentagon puts the war's cost at least $47.1 billion so far; keeping Gulf oil moving may need about $2-3 billion a month more.
  • Supply help — Europe will release 42 million barrels from reserves.

Goldman Sachs says oil's fair value is about $85 and the rest is a war premium.

One view is that the strait cannot stay disrupted for long because the US, China and Gulf buyers all need it open. The opposite view is that 2 to 4 tankers a day are being lost and there are not enough ships to replace them, so freight stays dear for a long time. Reading between the lines, the ship shortage may last longer than the oil shortage.

The refiners' windfall

Fuel is short, not just crude. Exxon's chief said the diesel and jet fuel gap comes a third each from the Gulf, China and the Russia-Ukraine war. So whoever owns refineries is earning record profits.

Russia, China and gas

  • Russia — refinery output rose about 25% when Ukraine paused drone strikes; Ukraine then broke the pause with a large attack. The US has suspended sanctions on Russian diesel until April 2027.
  • China — pays about $248 per 1,000 cubic metres for Russian gas, against $562 for other buyers, a discount of about 56%.
  • Europe — gas costs about 11 times the US price, and weather models point to an early polar-vortex break, which could bring an early cold spell.

War usually lifts gold. This time gold fell to about $3,900, roughly 30% below its January peak, and silver lost more than half its value.

04

India's market: record results, falling prices

A flood of strong quarterly results met a market short of money, and the Jio listing pulled cash away from everything else.

In short
  • Many Indian companies posted their best quarter ever, but their shares often fell anyway.
  • Small and very small companies were sold the most, because buyers had little spare cash.
  • The giant Jio share sale listed well but soaked up money from the rest of the market.

Good results, bad marks

More than 100 companies reported on 30 October alone. One tracker counted far more good results than bad on both 29 and 30 October. Yet many of the good ones fell, which one investor called "an exam where good results get bad marks".

The Jio listing

Jio Platforms raised ₹30,213 crore, India's largest share sale, and listed at ₹1,358, about 21% above its issue price. Mumbai was covered in its hoardings, and payment codes for the issue sat in Reliance shops.

  • Demand — bought 22.19 times over; value above ₹12.5 lakh crore at listing, and briefly above ₹13.5 lakh crore.
  • After — fell from about ₹1,488 to ₹1,384 within a session.
  • Index — Nomura expects entry into the Sensex by end-December and the Nifty by end-March, which brings forced buying by index funds.
  • Lock-in — 32.6% of shares held by early investors such as Meta and Google become free to sell on 26 April 2027.

One view is that Jio is a growing, cash-rich business with index buying ahead. The opposite view, put to Bloomberg and CNBC by one fund manager, is that at 14% yearly growth Jio would need about 20 years to match AT&T's revenue, so buyers are paying as if India's economy were already the size of America's. Reading between the lines, the issue pulled cash out of smaller shares in the days before it opened, which helps explain the October slide.

One view, from Bajaj Asset Management's Nimesh Chandan, is that the market is a barbell: small caps have the growth and large caps have the cheaper prices. The opposite view, from chart readers, is that after a peak it takes years for prices to recover, so cash is the right place for now. Both agree on one thing: this is a stock picker's market, not an index market.

05

India's economy: fast growth, thin pockets

The headline numbers are strong, but money leaving the country, slowing card spends and rising daily costs tell a quieter story.

In short
  • India's economy is still growing over 7% a year, and exports are on course for a record.
  • But most foreign investment that comes in also goes out again.
  • Prices of everyday items are rising, and people are spending less on credit cards.

Growth holds up

The Finance Ministry estimates growth of about 7.35% in the July-September quarter, after 7.8% in the quarter before. The Chief Economic Adviser says exports of goods and services are on course for $1 trillion this year.

Money in, money out

India drew a record $97 billion of gross foreign direct investment (FDI, money put into companies and plants) in FY26. But most of it left again, through foreign owners selling stakes in share sales and Indian firms investing abroad.

One view calls this a warning sign. The opposite view, from an industrial-policy writer, says the gloomy reading is wrong: net FDI in April-August hit $13.8 billion, a 4-year high, and exits are what happens when foreign owners earn returns. Both facts are true; the open question is whether new money keeps coming once exits slow.

India still imports more than it sells

India is the only big Asian economy that has run a trade deficit almost every year since 1991. Fuel is a large part of it: India's fuel imports move closely with its trade gap, a link of about 0.62 on a scale where 1 is perfect. So every rise in oil widens the gap and weakens the rupee, which links section 03 to section 02.

The household squeeze

  • Card spending — fell 5.9% from a year ago in September to ₹2.04 lakh crore, the first yearly fall in over 5 years; spend per card fell 14.8%.
  • Milk — Karnataka raised Nandini milk by ₹8 a litre from 1 November; hotels warned of dearer tea and food, and bus fares may follow.
  • Phones — Apple raised iPhone prices about 20-30% in India and festive iPhone sales fell up to 30%; memory costs have pushed phone prices up 35-40%.
  • Rural — Shriram Finance flagged stress from fuel costs and a weak monsoon; Maharashtra approved ₹7,500 crore of drought relief.
  • Debt habit — one widely shared report called India an "EMI nation", with people shopping on loans.

Part of the card drop is a high base: September 2025 had tax cuts and festive sales, and many small payments moved to UPI. Still, rich buyers are doing well while mass buyers are cutting back. Titan's jewellery head called it a "K-shaped" recovery, where the top 1% keep spending.
